The Boycott Leaflet

ARCHIVE COPY

A city begins to organize.

December 1955

Illustrative graphic · not the original leaflet

EXAMINE THE EVIDENCE / DOCUMENT 01

A leaflet dated December 2, 1955, called on Black Montgomery residents to avoid buses on the day of Rosa Parks’s trial. Click the source link below to view the actual historical item.

Historical context: Rosa Parks was arrested on December 1, 1955. Members of the Women's Political Council, led by Jo Ann Robinson, helped circulate a leaflet calling for Black residents of Montgomery to boycott the buses on December 5.

The leaflet reminds us that mass protest requires preparation, communication, and community networks. The boycott was not the work of a single person.

Source: Martin Luther King, Jr. Research and Education Institute, Stanford University, “Don't Ride the Bus,” December 2, 1955.

Volunteer drivers · Riders · Routes

Illustrative game prop · not a facsimile

EXAMINE THE EVIDENCE / DOCUMENT 02

Drivers, riders, and organizers needed clear plans for the daily trip to work. The paper record helps us see an organizing system rather than a single dramatic act.

A movement needs practical support. The Montgomery Improvement Association helped organize an extensive network of volunteer cars and drivers so residents could continue avoiding the segregated buses.

Rosa Parks's surviving papers include Instructions to car-pool drivers and passengers, 1955–1956. That document shows how even small daily details—routes, pickup points, and reliability—mattered to collective action.

Sociology connection: Collective action is sustained through resources, relationships, and cooperation—not only public speeches or widely known leaders.

Source: Library of Congress, Rosa Parks Papers, “Instructions to car-pool drivers and passengers, 1955–1956.” The original document is linked, not reproduced here.

EXAMINE THE EVIDENCE / FILE 03

On January 30, 1956, a bomb exploded at Martin Luther King Jr.'s home in Montgomery. His wife and infant daughter were inside and escaped injury.

The bombing did not end the boycott. Participants continued to organize transportation and sustain their protest despite threats.

Source: Joe Azbell, “Blast Rocks Residence of Bus Boycott Leader,” Montgomery Advertiser, January 31, 1956, preserved by the Martin Luther King, Jr. Research and Education Institute.

EXAMINE THE CASE / FILE 04

On February 21, 1956, a grand jury indicted boycott participants under Alabama's anti-boycott law. An initial 115 names were later reduced to 89.

Criminal prosecution versus a civil rights lawsuit. This file concerns Alabama's effort to prosecute boycott organizers. It is not the separate federal civil case, Browder v. Gayle, which challenged the constitutionality of bus segregation.

Sources: Martin Luther King, Jr. Research and Education Institute, indictment dated February 21, 1956, and court judgment and sentence dated March 22, 1956.

OPEN THE CASE FILE · FOUR PIECES OF EVIDENCE

Aurelia Browder, Claudette Colvin, Susie McDonald, and Mary Louise Smith were the four remaining plaintiffs. Jeanetta Reese was an original plaintiff but withdrew after facing pressure. Their lawsuit challenged state and city bus-segregation laws.

Two routes to change. The boycott sustained collective economic pressure while the plaintiffs, represented by civil rights attorney Fred Gray, challenged segregation in federal court. Their efforts were connected, but they were not the same legal proceeding.

THE FINAL ARCHIVE · THREE RECORDS

December 19, 1956: community leaders circulated guidance for using integrated buses. The guidance emphasized discipline and nonviolent conduct during a historic, uncertain transition.

A victory built by many people. The boycott relied on neighbors walking, scheduling rides, organizing in churches and civic groups, supporting families, and pursuing legal challenges. The change in bus-seating rules was an enormous achievement, but it did not resolve every injustice.

Primary sources: Martin Luther King, Jr. Research and Education Institute, “Integrated Bus Suggestions” (December 19, 1956) and “Statement on Ending the Bus Boycott” (December 20, 1956). Historical timeline also verified against the Institute’s chronology.
