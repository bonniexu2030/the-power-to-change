EXAMINE THE EVIDENCE / DOCUMENT 01

A leaflet dated December 2, 1955, called on Black Montgomery residents to avoid buses on the day of Rosa Parks’s trial. Click the source link below to view the actual historical item.

Historical context: Rosa Parks was arrested on December 1, 1955. Members of the Women's Political Council, led by Jo Ann Robinson, helped circulate a leaflet calling for Black residents of Montgomery to boycott the buses on December 5.

The leaflet reminds us that mass protest requires preparation, communication, and community networks. The boycott was not the work of a single person.

Source: Martin Luther King, Jr. Research and Education Institute, Stanford University, “Don't Ride the Bus,” December 2, 1955.

EXAMINE THE EVIDENCE / DOCUMENT 02

Drivers, riders, and organizers needed clear plans for the daily trip to work. The paper record helps us see an organizing system rather than a single dramatic act.

A movement needs practical support. The Montgomery Improvement Association helped organize an extensive network of volunteer cars and drivers so residents could continue avoiding the segregated buses.

Rosa Parks's surviving papers include Instructions to car-pool drivers and passengers, 1955–1956. That document shows how even small daily details—routes, pickup points, and reliability—mattered to collective action.

Sociology connection: Collective action is sustained through resources, relationships, and cooperation—not only public speeches or widely known leaders.

Source: Library of Congress, Rosa Parks Papers, “Instructions to car-pool drivers and passengers, 1955–1956.” The original document is linked, not reproduced here.
